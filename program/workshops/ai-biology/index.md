    <div>
      <h2>About the Workshop</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="section-body">
      <p>The rapid advancement in AI and machine learning is transforming how we study biological systems. Myriad biomedical fields, including genomics, drug discovery, protein engineering, and medical imaging, now rely heavily on AI. At the same time, biological problems are driving new methodological developments in AI, in areas like sequence modeling, explainable AI, causal learning, and representation learning over structured spaces.</p>
      <p>The AI and Biology workshop at AI/CRV 2026 brings together researchers working across this intersection. Topics of interest include genomics, single-cell omics and systems biology; protein engineering, synthetic biology and drug discovery; medical imaging and clinical data; as well as the underlying methodological challenges these domains present.</p>
    </div>

</section>

        <li><strong>Michael Hoffman (U Toronto, UHN):</strong> “Visualizing distortion in dimensionality reduction.”
          <p>Abstract: Dimensionality reduction makes high-dimensional data interpretable by projecting it into a lower-dimensional space. Widely used methods such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) preserve select geometric properties of the input data. For example, PCA preserves variance, while t-SNE and UMAP preserve local neighborhood structure. As a consequence, inter-point and inter-cluster distances in the embedding do not reliably reflect distances in the original high-dimensional space. Nevertheless, practitioners routinely interpret these distances as biologically meaningful, leading to systematic misinterpretations of the underlying data.</p>
          <p>To address this problem, we developed a visualization method that directly encodes distance distortion within dimensionality reduction plots. We overlay an evenly spaced grid on the embedded space and assign each grid point to its nearest data point. Each selected data point is then mapped back to its original high-dimensional coordinates, establishing paired positions in both spaces. We compute Euclidean distances between all sampled point pairs in each space and take their ratios (fold changes) as a measure of distortion. These fold changes are binned into quantiles, with each quantile assigned a distinct contour-line frequency, creating an interpretable overlay that reveals the magnitude of distance distortion across the embedding.</p>
          <p>We applied this method to the MNIST database, a PBMC 3k single-cell RNA-seq dataset, and a COVID immune atlas, comparing distortion patterns across PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP embeddings. Our results demonstrate that the method exposes otherwise hidden distance distortions, providing a practical safeguard against overinterpretation of inter-cluster distances in dimensionally reduced plots.</p>

          <p>Abstract: Researchers have identified the molecular fingerprints of many cancers and used them to divide cancer’s once-broad categories into far more precise types and subtypes. On the other hand, they have discovered that cancers that develop in different parts of the body may have a lot in common on a molecular level. This has motivated the new paradigm of precision oncology with the vision of diagnosing and treating patients more accurately and effectively based on their multi-omics (genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, . . .) profile. Tasks of precision oncology include patient stratification, diagnosis, prognosis, drug target identification and drug discovery. Realizing the potential of precision oncology requires the availability of multi-omics patient data and the development of machine learning methods to analyze these highly complex datasets.</p>
          <p>In this talk, we will give an overview of the machine learning methods for precision oncology that have been developed in the Ester lab, often in collaboration with colleagues at the VPC. For the task of drug response prediction, we have developed methods that integrate different modalities of omics data and methods of transfer learning from preclinical to clinical data. We have also proposed guidelines for the choice of training datasets and of suitable drug response metrics. While most machine learning methods are based on correlations between input features and outcomes, we have developed a range of methods for the discovery of causal relationships, focusing on the discovery of genetic causes of adverse drug reactions. scRNA-seq data enable the analysis of molecular data at the cell level instead of the aggregate level, and we have investigated clustering algorithms for cell-type identification and annotation as well as a model for phenotype prediction given a set of cells. Finally, for the task of drug discovery, we have developed methods for virtual screening, using a machine learning model as a proxy to predict binding affinity, and generative molecule models, generating molecules from scratch conditioned on a target protein. The talk will conclude with an outlook on the Canadian Precision Health Initiative, whose first, ongoing phase is involving the sequencing of 100K genomes, and whose second phase will aim to build AI-enabled tools to use that data to create health impacts.</p>
